The one thing to learn from this paper: in a market of autonomous agents that learn by gradient-based, no-regret methods, Nash equilibrium is a defensible prediction essentially only when the equilibrium is strict, and even then only locally. The authors push the variational-inequality and Lyapunov toolkit onto the discrete-time, projected dynamics that real pricing and bidding algorithms run, and obtain a narrow certification: strong variational stability plus a small enough learning rate buys asymptotic stability and a computable basin of attraction. Outside that class — mixed equilibria, weak equilibria, and the discretized versions of Cournot oligopoly, contests and auctions — learning cycles, spirals or stalls, and equilibrium existence predicts nothing.

1. What do the researchers do?

They treat a market of learning agents as a dynamical system on a constrained strategy space. Agents play a continuous game with compact convex strategy sets and concave own-payoffs; the central object is the game gradient F(x), the stacked vector of individual payoff gradients. Each agent runs projected gradient ascent (PGA), x_{t+1} = Proj_X( x_t + eta * F(x_t) ), a follow-the-regularized-leader algorithm and hence no-regret. Because auctions have discontinuous payoffs, one discretizes actions and works with the mixed extension of the finite game, so X is a product of probability simplices and the interesting equilibria sit on its faces or vertices.

Three layers are assembled: the variational-inequality characterization (in concave games x* is Nash iff <F(x), x - x> <= 0 on X); variational stability, <F(x), x - x> < 0 near x, which Remark 4.1 shows is exactly the statement that V(x) = ||x - x*||^2 is a Lyapunov function; and Lyapunov/LaSalle theory rebuilt for discrete-time projected systems on the simplex — a Lebesgue-null set that forces the subspace topology (Appendix A).

Three results matter. Prop. 5.1: if F is L-Lipschitz and x* is strongly variationally stable, <F(x), x - x> <= -alpha ||x - x||^2, then x* is asymptotically stable under PGA for eta < 2alpha/L^2. Prop. 5.2: every strict Nash equilibrium of a finite game is locally strongly variationally stable, hence locally asymptotically stable. Prop. 5.3: a unique strict equilibrium need not attract from almost all initial conditions. Worked examples run from the Tullock contest through Battle of the Sexes to Matching Pennies, which is monotone but not strictly* so and spirals outward.

2. Why does the literature care about this?

Pricing and bidding are increasingly delegated to algorithms, and the algorithmic collusion debate presumes we know what repeated interaction of such algorithms produces. Standard game theory assumes agents know the environment and coordinate on an equilibrium from the start; neither holds for a bandit-feedback pricing bot. Hart and Mas-Colell (2003) showed uncoupled dynamics need not converge to Nash at all; Mertikopoulos et al. and Cheung–Piliouras document cycles and chaos under regularized learning; Sanders, Farmer and Galla argue non-convergence is the norm in large games. Equilibrium existence is therefore not a prediction, and the operative question becomes: for which market games can we certify that learning agents reach the equilibrium we designed for?

3. Why is it a contribution and where does it fit?

It sits at the seam between the CS learning-in-games literature (Mertikopoulos–Zhou, Mertikopoulos–Sandholm, Cohen et al., Giannou et al.) and economics’ adaptive-dynamics tradition (Monderer–Shapley, Milgrom–Roberts, Hart–Mas-Colell). Four moves: it analyses discrete-time PGA directly rather than its continuous-time limit or the mirror-ascent formulations that sidestep the boundary via Fenchel coupling; it supplies stability theory on closed, possibly measure-zero domains; it makes the basin of attraction computable rather than merely existent, via non-expansiveness of the Euclidean projection; and it names the obstruction that matters for economics — discretization destroys the properties that guarantee convergence — pointing to set-valued stability and semicoarse correlated equilibria (Ahunbay–Bichler 2025), whose LP dual doubles as a Lyapunov certificate.

4. What is a key figure or theorem from the paper?

The pair Prop. 5.1 / Prop. 5.3 is the spine. The proof of 5.1 is three lines: take V(x) = ||x - x||^2, use non-expansiveness of the projection and the fact that x is a fixed point, and

Delta V(x) <= eta^2 ||F(x) - F(x)||^2 + 2eta<F(x), x - x> <= eta(etaL^2 - 2alpha)||x - x||^2 < 0 for eta < 2alpha/L^2.

Convergence is certified by a single inner-product condition plus a learning-rate cap. Prop. 5.3 marks the limit with an “extended matching pennies” 3x3 game whose first action is a strict equilibrium paying r: if both players start with weight on that action below (2/3)q / ((2/3)r + q), the projected gradient on that component stays negative forever, so play never reaches it. That region has positive measure — strictness is necessary but not sufficient for global convergence.

5. Why was this hard and not done before?

Three obstacles. First, projection breaks smoothness. Classical Lyapunov theory — and the economist’s habit of linearising and reading eigenvalues off the game Jacobian — needs an interior equilibrium of a smooth unconstrained flow, whereas on the simplex the dynamics are non-smooth exactly where the interesting equilibria live, since vertices are pure-strategy equilibria. Earlier work escaped either into continuous time (Nagurney–Zhang projected dynamical systems, needing monotonicity plus an inward-pointing regularity condition at the boundary) or into the dual space, neither yielding discrete-time step-size conditions or basins. Second, the simplex has empty interior in R^n, so “neighborhood”, “ball” and “open” must be re-laid before LaSalle applies. Third, the economics is hostile: discretization destroys monotonicity and strictness, first-price auction games are not monotone (Bichler et al. 2025), and the impossibility results rule out any general positive theorem.

6. Where would this not do well?

  • The positive results are thin relative to the framing. Strong variational stability is essentially localised strong monotonicity, for which convergence of fixed-point iterations was already standard in the VI literature; Prop. 5.2 layers a short norm argument on Cohen et al. (2017), and the “strict iff stable and attracting” characterisation belongs to Giannou et al. (2021). The contribution is synthesis, not new theory.
  • The framework fails on the advertised applications. Section 5.6 concedes it: a symmetric Cournot duopoly, strongly monotone and exponentially stable in continuous actions, becomes on a seven-point grid a game with three weak pure equilibria — multiplicity rules out monotonicity, weakness rules out strong variational stability, and no tool in the paper applies. Contests and auctions behave identically. The certified class is “strict equilibria of already well-behaved games”; ad auctions and retail pricing sit outside it.
  • Basin estimates are conservative and do not scale. The certified set lies strictly inside the true basin even in a 2x2 game, and had to be found numerically there. For a first-price auction discretized to 100 bids with five bidders, the sublevel-set computation over a product of 100-simplices is hopeless, and no complexity discussion is offered.
  • The behavioural model is convenient rather than realistic. The motivation is bandit feedback, yet the analysis assumes full gradient feedback, a fixed common step size, synchronous updates, no noise, and a static complete-information game. Crucially there are no types: auctions are Bayesian games, and nothing here speaks to convergence to a Bayes–Nash equilibrium. Nor is Euclidean projection what agents run — Exp3 and Q-learning use entropic geometry, and the non-expansiveness argument does not transfer.
  • The headline negative result is proved only in continuous time. Prop. 5.3 is established for the continuous-time flow “since the analysis in discrete-time is challenging” — not for the algorithm the rest of Section 5 is about.
  • Stability is not welfare. The cleanest globally stable example in the paper is the Prisoner’s Dilemma, whose stable equilibrium is the inefficient one. Despite promising guidance on “when regulatory intervention may be necessary”, no result connects convergence to markups, efficiency or collusion — the questions the algorithmic-pricing literature actually asks, and which are absent from the bibliography.

7. How should researchers cite / refer to this paper?

Treat it as a survey-plus-framework paper, not a source of new equilibrium results. Cite it for: the claim that under gradient/no-regret learning strictness of the Nash equilibrium is the operative condition for learnability (citing Mertikopoulos–Zhou 2019 and Giannou et al. 2021 alongside); the counterexample that a unique strict equilibrium need not be globally attracting (Prop. 5.3); the discretization pathology (Sec. 5.6), the cleanest citation for why “just discretize the auction and let algorithms learn” is not innocuous; and as an entry point to the VI-plus-Lyapunov toolkit. Do not cite it for empirical or policy claims about algorithmic collusion. Cite the arXiv version (arXiv:2506.18571, cs.GT) until a journal version appears, and note that it is distinct from Bichler’s solo paper “Agentic markets” in Electronic Markets (2026) — a search-theoretic analysis with a confusingly similar title.
