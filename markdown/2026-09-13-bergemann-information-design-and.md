**The one thing to learn from this paper:** written in quantile space, screening and persuasion are the same mathematical object — maximize a **linear functional subject to a majorization constraint**, solved by **concavifying** a weight function — and seeing that delivers a result neither problem shows alone: when the designer controls the allocation *and* the information, **pooling is always optimal**, even where each problem separately would call for full separation and full disclosure.

## 1. What the researchers do

They take the **Mussa–Rosen (1978)** screening problem — a monopolist sells qualities q to unit-demand buyers with utility v*q − p — and, following **Loertscher and Muir (2022)**, give the seller an *exogenous inventory* of qualities instead of a convex production technology. The move: work entirely with **quantile functions** on t in [0,1] — V(t) is the value of the t-th quantile buyer (the prior), Q(t) the quality of the t-th quantile good (the inventory), W(t) the quantile function of buyers' *posterior expected* values (the information structure), X(t) that of *expected* qualities sold (the mechanism).

An envelope argument gives two bilinear payoffs: consumer surplus U(W,X) = integral of (1−t)*X(t) dW(t) and revenue R(W,X) = integral of (1−t)*W(t) dX(t) + X(0)*W(0). The integrand of one is the integrator of the other, giving an **exchange symmetry** R(W,X) = U(X,W) when W(0) = 0 or X(0) = 0.

The payoff is that **feasibility decouples**. A mechanism is feasible iff X is *weakly* majorized by Q (the increasing convex order; a generalized **Border (1991)** condition in the auction reading). An information structure is feasible iff W is *strictly* majorized by V (Blackwell: mean-preserving contractions of values are mean-preserving spreads of quantiles). Neither constraint mentions the other object — unlike in value space, where what the mechanism can sell depends on the value distribution.

Three problems follow. (i) **Mechanism design**: maximize over X weakly majorized by Q, weight the pointwise revenue r_W(t) = W(t)*(1−t), inverse demand times quantity. (ii) **Information design**: maximize over W majorized by V, weight the **excess quality** e_X(t) = integral from t to 1 of (X(s) − X(t)) ds, the spare quality above quantile t (the *excess wealth order*). (iii) **Joint design**: over both — bilinear, two majorization constraints. Throughout, the symmetric N-bidder auction is the case Q(t) = t^(N−1).

## 2. Why the literature cares

Optimal mechanism design runs on **Myerson (1981)** ironing and virtual values; information design on **Kamenica–Gentzkow (2011)** concavification over posterior means. Both produce bunching and are solved geometrically; the resemblance has long been sensed but not stated. Digital platforms now hold *both* levers, so the joint problem matters. And the **robust predictions** agenda needs this technology: a metaphorical designer, run across all welfare weights, traces the (revenue, consumer surplus) pairs achievable under *some* information structure.

## 3. The contribution and where it sits

This is a **survey-cum-synthesis** for a volume that also carries Kleiner–Moldovanu–Strack on majorization and Brooks–Du on informational robustness. The contribution is integration: one envelope formula and one table classifying all four design problems by *weight function* and *majorization order* — e.g. allocation/revenue is (r_W, weak), information/revenue is (e_X, strict). It rests on **Kleiner, Moldovanu and Strack (2021)** and restates **Bergemann, Heumann, Morris, Sorokin and Winter (2022, AER:Insights)** and **Bergemann, Heumann and Morris (2026, JPE, "Screening with Persuasion")**. Usefully, their information design problem is *not* standard Bayesian persuasion: it is linear in values but **nonlinear in probabilities**, i.e. persuasion with **Yaari (1987)** dual preferences (Jewitt–Quigley's "conjugate persuasion"). Hence Myerson-style quantile ironing, not Kamenica–Gentzkow concavification, is the right tool.

## 4. The key theorem

**Theorem 3 (Optimality of Pooling).** Every solution (W*, X*) to the joint problem — maximize R(W,X) over W majorized by V and X weakly majorized by Q — consists of **pooling intervals only**. With Proposition 2 the optimum is monotone partitional with **common support**: W* and X* jump at the same quantiles, so signals sent and qualities offered are in bijection (revelation and taxation principles at once). In the running example V(t) = Q(t) = t^4 (five bidders), the optimum offers **two** qualities to **two** pools.

The proof idea is the part to remember. Pool qualities on a short interval [t1, t2]: the revenue loss is bounded by sigma_phi * sigma_q * (t2 − t1), with sigma_phi, sigma_q the conditional standard deviations of virtual values phi(t) = V(t) − V'(t)*(1−t) and of qualities — a **third-order** effect. Now also pool the *values*: the quality step (mu_q − Q(t1)) is priced at the pooled mean mu_v rather than V(t1), raising payments by (mu_v − V(t1))*(mu_q − Q(t1))*(1−t1), collected from the whole non-negligible mass (1 − t1) above t1. That is **second-order**, so full separation is never optimal.

Two supporting results. **Theorem 2** (optimal information by concavifying e_X) with **Proposition 1**: under an efficient mechanism an inventory with *increasing* hazard rate gives **no disclosure**, a *decreasing* one **full disclosure**; auctions have non-monotone hazard rate, hence **upper censorship** — reveal below a cutoff t*(N), pool above. **Theorem 4** traces the whole welfare frontier by upper and lower censorship, switching discontinuously where total surplus is maximized. And t*(N) = 0, 0.25, 0.46, 0.58, 0.81, 0.98 for N = 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 100, so N*(1 − t*(N)) stays in (1.79, 2.25): optimal disclosure **sustains competition among roughly two bidders at the top**, however large N is.

## 5. Why this was hard

Three obstacles. First, the natural state space is wrong: in value space the objective is linear in posterior means but *nonlinear in probabilities*, so the standard persuasion toolkit does not apply. Inverting to quantiles flips the constraint (contractions become spreads) and restores linearity, but inverts intuition — which is why the equivalence went unnoticed. Second, the enabling mathematics is recent: **Kleiner, Moldovanu and Strack (2021)** characterized the extreme points of both majorization sets, including the *weak* order mechanism design needs because a seller can discard goods. Third, the joint problem is **bilinear with two coupled constraints** — not a concavification problem — so the pooling theorem needs the order-of-magnitude argument, not a first-order condition.

## 6. Where this will not do well

- **Novelty.** By the authors' own framing this is a survey; Theorems 1–3 restate Loertscher–Muir, Kleiner–Moldovanu–Strack and Bergemann–Heumann–Morris (2026). New material is thin: the unified exposition, Theorem 4, the comparative statics in N.
- **The quantile reduction is fragile.** It rests on utility v*q, so expected quality is a sufficient statistic and types are one-dimensional. Multidimensional types, nonlinear utility, risk aversion or budget constraints break the envelope formula and the apparatus with it.
- **Private values only.** Buyers learn about their *own* value. Common and interdependent values — where the informationally robust literature operates — sit outside the framework, limiting the claimed bridge to robust mechanism design (Section 8.3 sketches a saddle point, then defers to unpublished work).
- **Symmetry is not innocuous.** The auction mapping needs symmetric bidders *and* symmetric mechanisms, yet Bergemann and Pesendorfer (2007) showed **asymmetric** information structures and auctions are optimal in the general problem: a real restriction, not a normalization.
- **Interpretive strain.** Why can a seller coarsen buyers' knowledge of their own tastes with full commitment, yet not price-discriminate directly on it? The paper flags this and defers — though its closing policy claim about "excessive information manipulation" rests on it.
- **Qualitative, not quantitative.** Theorem 3 says every interval is pooled, but nothing about how many pools or how coarse. The "two bidders" regularity is numerical, and describes the designer's optimum rather than an observable market prediction.
- **Acknowledged loose ends.** Uniqueness needs strict concavity off the pooling intervals, which the authors call "generic" while admitting they have not formalized it. Endogenous Mussa–Rosen production is asserted, not shown. And all of it is static monopoly with free, committed disclosure: no dynamics, no seller competition, no cost of information.

## 7. How to cite and refer to it

Cite as **Bergemann, D., T. Heumann and S. Morris (2026), "Information Design and Mechanism Design: An Integrated Framework," Cowles Foundation Discussion Paper 2494 / arXiv:2601.17267**. Refer to it as **the integrated quantile-and-majorization framework**: the right citation for screening and persuasion as linear programs under majorization constraints, for the exchange symmetry, for the censorship-traced welfare frontier, or for the "about two bidders" comparative static. For the underlying results cite the sources: **Kleiner, Moldovanu and Strack (2021, Econometrica)**, **Loertscher and Muir (2022, JPE)**, **Bergemann, Heumann, Morris, Sorokin and Winter (2022, AER:Insights)**, and **Bergemann, Heumann and Morris (2026, JPE)** for joint design. Treat this paper as the map, not the territory.
